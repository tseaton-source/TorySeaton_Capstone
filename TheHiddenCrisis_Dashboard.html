Young Adults Are the Eye of the Storm

Substance use and depression converge at exactly the same life stage. Americans aged 21–25 face the highest rates of both — a critical window for early intervention. Filter by year to see how trends shift.

Filter by year:
Substance Use & Depression Rates by Age Group
"At ages 21–25, over 58% report alcohol use, 26% marijuana use, and 26.7% nicotine vaping — the same window where depression rates peak at 17%. Notably, vaping spikes sharply in youth and young adults while cigarette use peaks later in life (35–49), revealing a generational shift in how nicotine is consumed."
What about cocaine, heroin, and fentanyl? Cocaine, heroin, and fentanyl each account for less than 1% of reported use across all age groups. While low in self-reported rates, these substances carry disproportionate health risk and are not evenly distributed across the lifespan in ways that would be visible on this chart. These substances are covered in detail in Chapter 1, where the self-reporting caveat also explains why actual prevalence is likely higher than what this data captures.
Source: NSDUH 2021–2024, SAMHSA · N=232,441

"Women are 66% more likely to seek mental health treatment than men — yet men report depression at substantially lower rates, suggesting a large population of undiagnosed and untreated men."
What about cocaine, heroin, and fentanyl? Cocaine, heroin, and fentanyl each account for less than 1% of reported use across both genders. While the gender difference in these substances is real, the rates are too small to display meaningfully alongside alcohol and marijuana on this chart. These substances are covered in Chapter 1, where the self-reporting caveat also explains why actual prevalence is likely higher than what this data captures.
Is this mental health treatment being sought because of substance use?The NSDUH does not directly establish causation between substance use and mental health treatment-seeking. However, SAMHSA research consistently finds that over 50% of individuals with a substance use disorder have a co-occurring mental health condition. The patterns observed here strongly suggest that for many respondents, mental health treatment may be directly related to — or precipitated by — their substance use.
Source: NSDUH 2022–2023, SAMHSA · N=115,774 · Note: MH treatment data unavailable for 2021 and 2024

Does Treatment Work? Less Than Half Complete It.

Of the 4.2 million treatment episodes recorded between 2021 and 2023, only 37.5% resulted in successful completion. The substance someone is being treated for significantly affects their chances of finishing.

Filter by year:
Overall Treatment Outcomes

"Alcohol treatment has the highest completion rate at 49.7% while heroin treatment has the lowest at 26.1%. The substance matters — and so does the system supporting recovery."

Not All Treatment Is Created Equal.

The type of treatment someone receives has a major impact on whether they complete it. Hospital-based detox programs have the highest completion rates while standard outpatient programs — the most common form of treatment — have among the lowest.

Filter by year:
Treatment Completion Rate by Type (%)
"Detox programs show completion rates of 73–77% while standard outpatient — the treatment most people receive — sits at only 28.1%. Access to the right type of treatment is not equal across income levels or communities."

Why People Didn't Seek Treatment
Cost is the #1 barrier to treatment.
One in three people who needed treatment and didn't get it cited cost as the reason. This is not a personal decision — it is a policy failure. Expanding insurance coverage, funding community treatment centers, and removing financial barriers would directly reduce this number.
"The data is clear. The solutions exist. The only thing missing is the political will to act."
